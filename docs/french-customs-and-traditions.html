French art of living

Discover famous French customs and Traditions

Learn about French traditions and the social habits that define one of the world's most vibrant cultures.

In short

  • The French calendar is built around shared celebrations: Candlemas (February 2), Easter, the Fête de la Musique (June 21), Bastille Day (July 14) and Christmas Eve.
  • Meals are long, multi-course and social, and the table is set with care.
  • Everyday rituals include the apéritif before dinner, the 4 pm goûter and the café gourmand.
  • Say "bonjour", use "vous" with strangers, and never rush a meal.

Heading to France soon?

Knowing a few local customs — how people greet each other, how a meal unfolds, which festivals fill the calendar — turns a good trip into a memorable one, and shows respect for the people you meet.

Whether you stroll through Paris, explore smaller cities and villages, or order a croissant at a café, these are the traditions and table rituals worth knowing before you go.

Festive Traditions

France's celebrations mix history and food, and each region adds its own customs.

Bastille Day

Bastille Day, on July 14, marks the storming of the Bastille prison in 1789, the start of the French Revolution. Paris has its military parade and fireworks, and the regions celebrate in their own way:

  • Provence: traditional bull runs through the streets.
  • Corsica: fireworks with a Mediterranean feel.
  • Brittany: parades in Breton costume, crêpes and cider.
  • Basque Country: traditional dances and pelota in the town squares.

French Christmas and its Markets

Noël centres on the Réveillon, the Christmas Eve dinner: foie gras, smoked salmon, oysters, roast capon or turkey, and champagne. Dessert is the Bûche de Noël, a cake shaped like a yule log. Christmas markets, first a tradition of eastern France, now fill towns across the country with crafts, mulled wine and sweets.

Candlemas

La Chandeleur, on February 2, began as a religious feast and became the day of crêpes. Families flip them holding a coin in the other hand: a clean flip promises a prosperous year. Toppings are simple — sugar, lemon, chocolate or jam.

Easter

Pâques brings families together around roast lamb (agneau pascal) and spring vegetables, while chocolatiers fill their windows with eggs, bells, rabbits and hens. The legend says church bells fly to Rome on Good Friday and return on Easter Sunday, dropping chocolates for children to find in the garden.

Fête de la Musique

Created in 1982 by the French Ministry of Culture, the Fête de la Musique on June 21 marks the summer solstice. Streets, parks and squares become free stages for amateur and professional musicians of every genre.

Tour de France

First raced in 1903, the Tour de France runs every July over more than 2,000 miles of daily stages. The overall leader wears the yellow jersey; millions line the roads, and a caravan of promotional floats hands out freebies before the riders pass.

Culinary Traditions

A few clichés about French food, and the everyday rituals that are very real.

The French eat snails and frog legs

They do, but mostly on special occasions or in restaurants. A croissant or a baguette is a far more typical daily sight.

The French live on wine, baguettes and cheese

All three are loved and of excellent quality, but French cooking is far more varied: bouillabaisse in Marseille, cassoulet in the southwest, and hundreds of regional specialties.

The French spend hours at the table

Largely true. Long meals go back to the nobility's multi-course feasts, which the bourgeoisie later adopted. A full meal still runs starter, main, cheese, salad and dessert, with wine at each stage and plenty of conversation. Read more in our guide to the French table setting.

The art of French table setting

In l'art de la table, each glass and utensil is placed in the order the dishes will arrive, alongside linen, candles and flowers. A carefully set table is a sign of respect for guests.

The "Café Gourmand"

Born in 1985, the café gourmand is an espresso served with miniature desserts — a tiny crème brûlée, a mini chocolate mousse, a macaron or a fruit tartlet. It lets you have coffee and dessert without choosing. See our selection of authentic French desserts.

The open market

The weekly market is where locals choose their produce, chat with vendors and pick up fresh bread, brioche or a Breton kouign amann. It is as much a social outing as a shopping trip.

Snack time or the 4 pm "goûter"

The goûter (pronounced "goo-tay", from the verb goûter, "to taste") is the afternoon snack: pain au chocolat, bread with butter and jam, a madeleine with hot chocolate. A childhood ritual that adults keep too.

The tradition of the apéritif

The apéro (from the Latin aperire, "to open") is a glass of wine, Pastis or Kir before dinner, with olives, cheese, charcuterie and nuts. It opens the evening and slows it down. Santé!

Conclusion

From national celebrations like Bastille Day and Noël to daily rituals like the goûter and the long family meal, French customs share one idea:

Live in the moment, make it beautiful, and enjoy every minute of it.

FAQ

What are the dos and don'ts of French social etiquette?

Say "bonjour" when you walk into a shop and "au revoir" when you leave. Use "vous" with strangers and elders. Be on time for formal meetings; a few minutes late is fine for dinner at someone's home. Avoid asking about salary, don't rush a meal, and greet with a handshake — or la bise, the cheek kiss, among friends. A few words of French always help.

Are French people really that much into fashion?

Paris is a fashion capital, but everyday French style is understated: well-cut basics, neutral colors, jeans and a tailored jacket, chosen for comfort and to last.
